Emergency Numbers: Police, Ambulance and Fire for 50 Countries, Offline

29 October 2026 · by the developer of Orbitika

Short answer: Emergency Numbers is a tool inside Orbitika that shows the police, ambulance and fire numbers for 50 countries. Choose a country from the list and the three numbers appear in large red type, each with a copy button. The numbers are stored inside the app, so it is designed to work without Wi-Fi or mobile data. It is part of the Emergency tools in Orbitika Premium.

Quick test: what is the emergency number in the country you are standing in right now? Easy. Now what is it in the country you are flying to next month? Most of us freeze at that second question, and a quick search is the last thing we want to rely on when we have no signal and our hands are shaking.

I built Emergency Numbers because the right number is one of the smallest pieces of knowledge that matters most, and it changes the moment you cross a border. New Zealand is 111, Australia is 000, the United States is 911. If you grew up with one of those, your instinct is wrong almost everywhere else. I wanted the answer to be one tap away, before the panic, and with no internet needed.

What it does

You open the tool (the title bar says EMERGENCY NUMBERS) and you see a “Select country” box with a dropdown. Choose a country and the panel below it shows the country’s name and three rows: Police, Ambulance and Fire. Each row has a red icon, the number in large red numbers and a small copy button on the right.

Tap the copy button and the number goes to your clipboard. A short “Copied!” message confirms it, and you can paste the number straight into your phone’s dialler. At the bottom of the panel there is a small reminder: only call in a real emergency.

50 countries, in alphabetical order

The list runs from Argentina to Vietnam and covers a wide spread of places people actually travel to or have family in: Argentina, Australia, Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Canada, China, Croatia, the Czech Republic, Denmark, Egypt, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hong Kong, Hungary, India, Indonesia, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Kenya, Malaysia, Mexico, Morocco, Nepal, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, the Philippines, Poland, Portugal, Russia, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, South Africa, South Korea, Spain, Sri Lanka, Sweden, Switzerland, Taiwan, Tanzania, Thailand, Turkey, the UAE (Dubai), the United Kingdom, the United States and Vietnam.

Three kinds of country

When I put the list together, the countries fell into three patterns, and each one tells you something about how to read the screen.

  • One number for everything (16 countries). Australia (000), Canada (911), Denmark (112), Finland (112), Hong Kong (999), Ireland (999), Malaysia (999), Mexico (911), the Netherlands (112), New Zealand (111), the Philippines (117), Poland (112), Portugal (112), Sweden (112), the United Kingdom (999) and the United States (911). You see the same number three times, which is the easy case.
  • Two numbers (11 countries). Often police has its own number and ambulance and fire share one. Germany is 110 for police and 112 for ambulance and fire. Japan, South Korea and Taiwan share 119 for ambulance and fire, with police on a different line. Singapore uses 999 for police and 995 for ambulance and fire. Belgium, Kenya, Morocco, South Africa, Sri Lanka and Tanzania also fall here.
  • Three different numbers (23 countries). France is 17 for police, 15 for ambulance and 18 for fire. Italy is 112, 118 and 115. Spain uses 091, 061 and 080. Thailand lists 191 for police, 1669 for ambulance and 199 for fire. Egypt, Turkey, Brazil, India, Vietnam and the UAE (Dubai) are in this group too.

That last group is the reason I made the tool. If you only remember “112” from a holiday guide, you can still be wrong about which line sends an ambulance in a particular country, and it’s easier to read the screen than to guess.

Why it’s offline

Like everything in Orbitika, Emergency Numbers is designed to work without Wi-Fi or mobile data. The numbers are written into the app, not fetched from a website, so there is nothing to load and no account to sign in to. That matters because the times you need an emergency number are often the times your signal is weakest: a hike, a long drive, a ferry, a trip abroad with roaming switched off.

Who it helps

Travellers who change country every few days, students on exchange, people who work with international groups, and anyone with family overseas. It’s also useful for hosts and organisers: if you’re the person responsible for a group trip, you can look up the numbers for the places you’re visiting before you leave. And it’s for the rest of us who would simply prefer to look it up than to trust our memory.

A few tips

  • Look up your destination before you go. Do it once at home, when you’re calm, and note the numbers for anyone travelling with you.
  • Check which countries are in the “three numbers” group. Those are the ones where it pays to read the screen rather than rely on a single remembered number.
  • Use the copy button to avoid mistyping. Pasting the number into the dialler is faster than typing it, and it removes a chance of a slip when you’re stressed.
  • Pair it with the first aid guide. Knowing the number and knowing what to do while you wait go together. I wrote about that in the First Aid Guide post.

An honest limit

Emergency Numbers copies the number; it does not dial it for you, so you still make the call yourself. The tool opens on Argentina, the first country in the list, so you need to choose your own country each time you open it, and there is no search box, only the dropdown.

The numbers are stored in the app and are not checked live, which means they are only as up to date as the version you have installed. If you are heading somewhere unusual or somewhere where the system has recently changed, confirm the number with a local source as well. The tool covers police, ambulance and fire only. It does not know about poison lines, mountain rescue or other specialist services.

Frequently asked questions

How many countries does Emergency Numbers cover?

Fifty, from Argentina to Vietnam. For each country it shows the police, ambulance and fire numbers.

Does Emergency Numbers work offline?

Yes. The numbers are stored inside the app, so it is designed to work without Wi-Fi or mobile data.

Can it call the number for me?

No. It copies the number to your clipboard and you dial it yourself.

Does it open on my country?

No. It opens on Argentina, the first country in the list, and you choose your own country from the dropdown.

Is Emergency Numbers free?

No. It is part of the Emergency tools in Orbitika Premium, a one-time purchase with no subscription.
