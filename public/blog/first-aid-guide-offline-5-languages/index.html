First Aid Guide: Seven Emergencies, Five Languages, No Internet Needed

29 October 2026 · by the developer of Orbitika

Short answer: The First Aid Guide in Orbitika gives numbered steps for seven emergencies: CPR, Bleeding, Choking, Burns, Fracture, Drowning and Heat Stroke. You can read them in English, Chinese, Hindi, Japanese or Korean. The guide is stored inside the app, so it is designed to work without Wi-Fi or mobile data. It is educational only and is part of the Emergency tools in Orbitika Premium.

Most of us know first aid exists. Far fewer of us can remember the order of the steps when someone is on the floor and everyone is looking at us. I’ve noticed that the hardest part isn’t the knowledge, it’s the first ten seconds, when your mind goes blank and you can’t remember whether you cool a burn with ice or not.

I built the First Aid Guide so the answer is one tap away, in short numbered steps, in a language the person next to you can read. It isn’t a course and it doesn’t pretend to be one. It’s a prompt for when your memory lets you down.

What it does

At the top of the screen there is a Language box with a dropdown. Under it is a row of seven coloured topic buttons. Tap a topic and the panel below fills with numbered steps. Under the steps you’ll see a short warning for that topic, then a disclaimer, and at the very bottom a reminder to call your local emergency number.

The seven topics

Here is what each one covers, in the order the buttons appear. These are summaries of what is in the guide, not a replacement for it.

  • CPR: check responsiveness by tapping and shouting, call emergency services, open the airway by tilting the head back, and check breathing by looking, listening and feeling for up to 10 seconds. Then start chest compressions with your hands on the centre of the chest, pushing hard and fast about 5–6 cm deep at 100–120 compressions a minute, give rescue breaths, and continue until help arrives. The warning says it is for adults only and that child CPR uses a different ratio.
  • Bleeding: stay calm and reassure the person, press directly on the wound with a clean cloth, and raise the wound above heart level. If blood soaks through, add more cloth and do not remove the first layer. Apply a pressure bandage, call emergency services if the bleeding is severe, and watch for signs of shock: pale, cold, dizzy.
  • Choking: ask “Are you choking?” and encourage coughing if they can speak or cough. If they can’t speak, the steps describe the Heimlich manoeuvre: stand behind, make a fist above the navel, grasp it with the other hand and thrust upwards. If the person becomes unconscious, start CPR. The warning notes that for infants you should use back blows and chest thrusts instead.
  • Burns: remove the person from the heat source, cool the burn with cool (not cold) running water for at least 10–20 minutes, remove jewellery and tight clothing from the area, and cover with a sterile dressing or cling film. The guide says not to use ice, butter or ointments, and to call emergency services for major burns.
  • Fracture: avoid moving the person unless necessary, keep the injured area still and supported, apply a cold pack that doesn’t touch bare skin, and immobilise with a splint. Cover an open fracture with a sterile dressing, call emergency services immediately and do not try to straighten the bone.
  • Drowning: make sure it is safe before you enter water, get the person out as soon as possible, check responsiveness, and call emergency services if there’s no response. Open the airway, check breathing, and if they aren’t breathing start CPR with 2 rescue breaths first, then 30 chest compressions, continuing at 2 breaths to 30 compressions. If they vomit, roll them onto their side. The warning says to seek medical attention even if the person seems fine.
  • Heat Stroke: call emergency services immediately, move the person into cool shade, remove excess clothing and cool them rapidly, using cool water, ice packs on the armpits, groin, neck and back, or spray and a fan. Don’t give fluids if they’re unconscious; if they’re conscious, give cool (not cold) water. Monitor breathing and consciousness, and keep cooling until help arrives.

Five languages

The language box offers English, Chinese, Hindi, Japanese and Korean. Switch the language and the screen title, the topic buttons, the steps, the disclaimer and the footer all change. I added this because emergencies don’t only happen to people who share your first language: a friend, a relative or a stranger might be the one who needs to read the steps while you do the first aid. You can hand them the phone.

Why it’s offline

Every step and every translation is stored inside the app. The First Aid Guide is designed to work without Wi-Fi or mobile data, so there’s no loading page and no search results to scroll through. That matters on a hiking trail, at a beach, on a long drive or abroad with roaming off.

Who it helps

Parents and grandparents, hikers and campers, people who work alone or with groups, travellers, and anyone who lives or works alongside Chinese, Hindi, Japanese or Korean speakers. It’s also a useful refresher for people who did a first aid course a few years ago and want a quick reminder of the order.

A few tips

  • Read the topics once when you’re calm. You’ll recognise the steps faster if you’ve seen them before, and you can notice which ones you didn’t know.
  • Read the warning line under each topic. That’s where it says, for example, that CPR here is for adults and that infants who are choking need a different approach.
  • Call for help first or ask someone else to. Nearly every topic includes “call emergency services”, so if someone is with you, get them to make the call while you start. The Emergency Numbers tool lists the numbers for 50 countries.
  • Use the language switch to include others. If a bystander reads another language, change the setting and let them follow the steps too.

An honest limit

The guide is for educational purposes only, and the app says so on every screen: always seek professional medical help in an emergency. A certified first aid course will teach you far more than any app, and I’d encourage you to take one, especially for CPR, where hands-on practice matters.

One detail to know: the short warning line under each topic is written in English and stays in English even when you switch the rest of the screen to another language. The steps, titles, disclaimer and footer are translated, but that line isn’t yet.
