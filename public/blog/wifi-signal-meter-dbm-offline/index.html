Your phone shows a little Wi-Fi symbol with a few curved lines. That’s all it says: “some” or “a lot”. If you’ve ever carried a laptop around the house looking for the corner where the video stops buffering, you know it’s not enough. What you really want is a number you can watch change as you walk.

That’s the WiFi Signal tool. It reads the strength of the network you’re connected to and shows it in a retro gauge that updates as you move.

What it does

You open the tool (the title bar says WIFI SIGNAL) and see three things: the network name at the top, a big dBm number inside a gauge, and a quality label under it, with four signal bars.

  • Network name: shown at the top once you’ve allowed location access. Until then, a button says “TAP TO SHOW NETWORK”.
  • dBm number: a large reading such as −52, with “dBm” beneath it.
  • Quality label: EXCELLENT, GOOD, FAIR, WEAK or VERY WEAK, matching the four bars.
  • Gauge: a three-quarter arc that fills as the signal gets stronger.

How to read the number

A note at the bottom of the screen explains it: “Closer to 0 is better. −30 is excellent, −80 is poor.” The gauge is scaled between about −100 dBm (empty) and −30 dBm (full), so you can see at a glance where you are.

It keeps updating

The tool refreshes by itself every 3 seconds, so you can walk around and watch the number move. A refresh button in the corner updates it on demand. When you put the app in the background, the updates pause, and they resume when you return.

When there’s no signal

If your phone isn’t connected to Wi-Fi, the tool says NO WIFI. If it can’t read the network it says COULD NOT READ WIFI, and if you decline the location permission it says PERMISSION DENIED. Where the network name can’t be read, a “TAP TO SHOW NETWORK” button lets you try again.

Why it needs no internet

Measuring signal strength is something your phone does locally, so the tool doesn’t need internet access, only a Wi-Fi connection. That means it works even when the router is connected but the internet is down, which is exactly when you want to know whether the problem is the signal or the provider.

Who it helps

People setting up a router, remote workers choosing the best spot in a house, travellers trying a hotel or café network, students in a library, and anyone wondering why a video call keeps freezing.

A few tips

  • Walk slowly and watch the number. Wait a few seconds in each spot, since it updates every 3 seconds.
  • Aim for −67 or better for video calls. That’s a common rule of thumb, though your needs may differ.
  • Test near the router first, then move away to see how fast the signal drops.
  • Try moving the router. Higher up and away from walls or metal often helps.

An honest limit

The tool measures the signal strength of the network you’re connected to, not its speed. A strong signal doesn’t guarantee fast internet, and it doesn’t list nearby networks or tell you which channel is busiest. Reading the network name needs location permission, which is a requirement of the phone, not something the tool uses for anything else.
